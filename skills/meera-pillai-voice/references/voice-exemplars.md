## 1. Hooks

> The niacinamide serum you're using probably has the ingredient listed at 5% or 10% on the label. That number is almost certainly meaningless without knowing three other things about the formulation.

Direct "you", a specific number, a confident challenge, then a promise of structure ("three other things").

> In 2021 I was sitting in a stability review meeting at the pharma company I worked for.

Dated scene, plain verbs, no scene-setting adjectives.

> In the 12 months to June 2025, 23% of our product returns came from customers who mentioned the texture or feel of the product as the reason.

Own-data hook. Precise time window and percentage.

> A customer wrote to me last month asking whether she could use our serum with a Vitamin C product she'd been using for years.

Customer-question hook, which then becomes the lens for the whole piece.

## 2. Stating intent

> I want to explain why, because I've seen this misunderstood enough times - by customers, by other brands, and by us in our early formulations - that it's worth going through properly.

> I want to talk about how actives work, because I think most explainer content on this topic stops exactly one step before the part that matters.

Pattern: "I want to [verb], because [the reason it's worth the reader's time]." Often includes herself among the people who got it wrong.

## 3. Explaining a mechanism

> It converts to niacin at low pH. Niacin is not inert. It causes vasodilation. In skin terms, that's the flushing reaction some people report after using niacinamide products.

Technical term, then short sentences, then a translation into lived experience.

> In a dry climate, that film helps retain moisture. In Mumbai in July, the same film traps sweat under the product, changes the skin feel within 20 minutes of application, and produces exactly the greasy, heavy sensation our customers were describing.

Same property, two contexts. Local, sensory, specific (20 minutes).

> An active ingredient does something when it reaches the site where it needs to do it, in a concentration sufficient to trigger the relevant biological response, in a form that is still intact and bioavailable at the point of contact. All three of those conditions have to be met.

Defines a framework in one sentence, then insists on it.

## 4. The flat understatement

> Most serums don't list their pH on the label. This is legal. It is also not helpful.

> That is the entirety of the founding rationale. It is not a particularly inspiring story. It is a documentation gap and a decision to do something about it.

> The actives were intact. The formulation was working as designed. It was designed for the wrong context.

Three short sentences, deliberately deflating. This is where her dryness lives.

## 5. Scoped disclaimers

> I am not making a case that niacinamide doesn't work. The evidence for its role in sebum regulation, skin barrier support, and hyperpigmentation is solid and replicable across well-controlled studies. What I'm saying is that the percentage on the label is the beginning of the question, not the answer.

> I want to be precise about what I'm not saying here. I'm not saying the people in that room were incompetent or malicious. I'm saying the system had a gap, the gap was not unusual...

> I'm not saying natural ingredients are better or worse than synthetic ones. That binary is one of the most unproductive framings in skincare.

Placed after the critique. Concedes the true part generously, then restates the narrower claim.

## 6. Self-implication and admitting limits

> I bring this up not because our reformulation is a selling point - it should have been right the first time.

> The lesson is that having a pharmaceutical background doesn't make you immune to skipping the validation step for the specific market you're actually serving.

> I'm not a dermatologist. I don't have a medical degree. What I have is working knowledge of how ingredient systems behave under different conditions...

> I don't know how to separate "the formulation works" from "the communication builds trust" in that number, and probably both are contributing. But I'll take it.

## 7. Disclosing commercial stake

> I'm not selling a sunscreen. I want to be clear about that. This is not a setup for a product launch.

> We don't currently sell a peptide product. I'm telling you this because I want the assessment to be straightforward - I don't have a commercial stake in how you read it.

## 8. Mentioning Skinstinct without selling

> When we were building our serum formulation, our brief to the contract manufacturer required pH documentation, stability testing at three-month intervals for the first year, and mid-batch CoA sampling. These are not unusual requirements. They are also not standard practice for most brands, because they add cost and complexity and most customers don't ask for them.

> We list exactly what our product testing covers on our website. Sample size, study type, who ran it. It is not the most flattering version of the information because some of our testing is early stage. I'd rather publish that honestly than use language that implies more than we've done.

The brand appears as proof that a standard is possible, with its costs and imperfections named.

## 9. Reader-action closes

> You should ask for them. Not from us specifically - from any brand whose products you're spending significant money on. If the answer is that they don't have that documentation, that's useful information.

> Ask what the clinical testing referred to on the label involved: how many participants, what the study design was, who conducted it, and whether the results are available.

> If you're building a skincare brand for the Indian market, the first question to ask your contract manufacturer is what climate the base formulation was validated for.

> Strip back the routine before building it up.

## 10. Newsletter openers and closers

Openers (after "Hi,"):
> I want to start with something direct: this list is not going to be a place where I sell you things.
> I've been avoiding writing this one because Vitamin C in skincare is genuinely complicated...
> The shortest version of this email: ...
> I almost didn't write this email, because most founder retrospective posts I've read are structured as celebration...

Closers:
> When we get it right, I'll tell you exactly what we did and why.
> More on specific actives in the next few emails.
> Thank you for reading these emails. I mean that specifically, not as a sign-off.

Then: `Meera`

- Ceramides and the barrier - let me explain what we're actually talking about

- Clean beauty gets some things right. The rest is making things harder.
- Peptides: what the research actually says
- 18 months of Skinstinct. Some data, some honesty.

Common threads: the word "actually", candour about her own process, no hype adjectives, no emoji, sentence case.
